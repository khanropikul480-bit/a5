Constant-Force Escapements & Remontoire Mechanisms in Haute Horlogerie

By Senior Horological Fellows • Published: Chronometer Monograph Series • Observatory Precision Research

The Ubiquitous Problem of Torque Decay in Mechanical Watchmaking

In the vast majority of mechanical wristwatches, motive power originates in a coiled carbon or alloy mainspring housed inside the mainspring barrel. By its fundamental physics (Hooke's law of elasticity), a coiled spring exerts its maximum rotational torque when fully wound, steadily decaying across its 40 to 72 hours of autonomy. As torque degrades, the balance wheel's rotational amplitude drops from a healthy 300 degrees down to 220 degrees or lower.

This amplitude variation drastically alters the escapement's contact timing, causing the watch to gain time initially and lose time as reserve expires. Furthermore, manufacturing imperfections in the teeth of the four-wheel gear train introduce cyclical torque ripples (backlash noise) that disturb the balance wheel. To achieve true observatory-grade precision, the regulating organ must be physically isolated from the gear train.

The Remontoire d'Égalité: A Secondary Reservoir of Pure Force

Conceived in the 15th century by clockmaker Jost Bürgi and perfected by English genius John Harrison during his legendary Marine Chronometer trials, the Remontoire d'Égalité (constant-force remontoire) introduces a secondary micro-power source placed as close as possible to the escapement.

In a one-second wrist remontoire, the main gear train does not drive the escape wheel directly. Instead, the gear train drives a winding star that winds up a tiny spiral hairspring mounted on the escape wheel arbor. Every second, an escape pallet releases a stop-finger, allowing the main gear train to advance and recharge the remontoire spring. The escape wheel itself is powered solely by this unvarying micro-spring, receiving an unshakeable, mathematically identical pulse of energy every single second of operation.

Gravity-Arm Escapements in Astronomical Regulators

While spring remontoires represent the zenith of wristwatch engineering, astronomical floor regulators utilize an even purer constant-force mechanism: the Gravity-Arm Escapement, perfected by Lord Grimthorpe in the Great Clock of Westminster (Big Ben) and refined by Sigmund Riefler.

In a gravity escapement, the pendulum never touches the escape wheel or gear train. Instead, the gear train simply lifts a small, precisely weighted gravity arm by a fixed fraction of a millimeter and locks it. As the pendulum swings through its center, it unlatches the gravity arm, which falls under the unvarying influence of Earth's gravitational acceleration (g), delivering a gentle kinetic impulse to the pendulum rod before resetting.

Because Earth's gravity is constant, the impulse energy delivered to the pendulum is identical whether the driving weights have just been wound or are resting at the bottom of the case. Gear train friction, oil thickening, and gear-tooth imperfections are 100% quarantined from the pendulum, delivering astronomical timekeeping that endures through centuries.

The Geometry of the One-Second Geneva Stop Re-Wind Cam

The mechanical heart of our wristwatch remontoire is the five-tooth star wheel and anchor stop-finger mechanism. Mounted on the fourth wheel arbor, the stop-finger rests against a synthetic ruby lock jewel. As the balance wheel oscillates through five complete vibrations (at a frequency of 18,000 vibrations per hour), the remontoire spiral hairspring uncoils by exactly 72 degrees.

At the fifth vibration, a micro-cam lifts the stop-finger for exactly 12 milliseconds. The gear train spins forward, propelled by the mainspring, re-winding the remontoire hairspring back to its starting tension before the stop-finger drops onto the next jewel tooth. The transition is so rapid and smooth that the balance wheel experiences zero impulse fluctuation, maintaining a perfectly linear power curve across the entire 72-hour operating reserve.

Visual Drama: The Deadbeat Jumping Seconds Display

An extraordinary horological byproduct of the one-second remontoire is the authentic deadbeat seconds display (seconde morte). Unlike standard quartz watches where a stepper motor jerks the second hand with harsh electrical stops, the mechanical deadbeat second hand on our Sovereign Wrist Regulator jumps with organic mechanical grace.

The hand remains completely motionless for 988 milliseconds, then darts forward in a lightning-fast 12-millisecond leap to the next minute marker, stopping dead without bounce or overshoot thanks to an integrated inertial damper. It provides an immediate visual confirmation of constant-force regulation, turning the passage of time into an enthralling mechanical ballet on the wrist.

Kinetic Analysis of Remontoire Torque Curves and Energy Distribution

In an uncontrolled horological gear train, mainspring torque decreases progressively from full wind to zero according to Hooke's law of elasticity. This mechanical torque gradient results in variable impulse energy imparted to the balance wheel or deadbeat pendulum, altering the amplitude of oscillation by several degrees. Because neither spring balances nor pendulums are purely isochronous across wide amplitude swings, this decay introduces noticeable rate fluctuations over a 48-hour power reserve.

The incorporation of a secondary spring remontoire—wound once every one second or once every sixty seconds by the primary barrel—decouples the escapement entirely from the gear train. The escape wheel only interacts with the micro-wound remontoire spiral. Measurements conducted in the PendulumCraft Mercer Street laboratory demonstrate that the impulse variation drop across a 72-hour cycle is reduced from ±14.2% down to less than ±0.18%. This produces a virtually flat horizontal rate curve, fulfilling the supreme objective of haute horlogerie precision timekeeping.
